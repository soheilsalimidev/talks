    FULL TALK CONTENT (your knowledge base):
    PART 1 — INTRODUCTION:
    - Adaptive learning systems solve one core problem: finding the right difficulty for each learner. Too hard → frustration, too easy → boredom, just right → learning (the flow channel).
    - Arthur example: Arthur keeps missing 42 − 17. KT sees he is solid on place value and single-digit facts but shaky on regrouping (borrowing). It serves two quick regrouping items, retries 42 − 17, and flags him to the teacher before the unit quiz.
    - Without KT: same sequence for everyone, struggling students get harder items and give up, strong students check out, instructor only sees the final score. With KT: mastery tracked per concept on every answer, next item in the flow channel, finds the missing prerequisite instead of just "wrong", early warning before failure.
    - Applications: adaptive platforms (Duolingo, Khan Academy, MOOCs), tutoring chatbots / ITS, early-warning systems, personalized exercise generation, instructor dashboards. Common thread: anywhere a system must decide "what should this learner do next?"
    - KT in one picture: Input (correctness logs, and now text conversations thanks to LLMs) → KT Model (infers the latent state) → Output (e.g., P(master skill k) = 0.72) → Action (recommend / intervene). Hard because knowledge is unobservable, one question can need several skills, skills have prerequisites, and learners forget.
    PART 2 — CURRENT SOLUTIONS & LIMITATIONS:
    - Three camps: (1) Probabilistic — BKT, ~2000s, binary mastery per skill, simple/interpretable/fast. (2) Deep Learning — DKT, SAKT, AKT, 2015–2023, RNNs/Transformers over logs, learns embeddings from scratch. (3) LLM-based — 2023–now, this review, reads question text, reasons/generates/explains, generalizes across domains.
    - What's broken: BKT assumes one skill per question and binary knowledge → too rigid. Deep KT has cold-start — IDs learned from logs, so new questions or new students are invisible, and it ignores what the question says. Both are not interpretable, need big structured logs, and can't diagnose why an answer was wrong. Educational datasets are orders of magnitude sparser than recommendation-domain data.
    - The LLM promise: semantics (infer skills and difficulty from text), generalization (unseen questions, subjects, platforms), interpretability (explain a prediction in language an instructor reads), generation (hints, feedback, new practice questions on demand).
    PART 4 — FINDINGS (from 44 studies, organized by theme):
    - Finding 1 · RQ1 — Five roles LLMs play in KT: (1) Semantic Extractor — encodes question text and feeds a separate KT backbone (most common); (2) Auxiliary Predictor — estimates difficulty / tags concepts for unseen items (very common); (3) Student Simulator — generates plausible wrong answers to augment data (rare, growing); (4) Standalone Tracer — the LLM itself predicts the next response (contested); (5) Hybrid Component — LLM + sequential model, each covering the other's weakness (rare, growing).
    - Finding 2 · RQ2 — Four ways to wire an LLM in: (1) Prompting — serialize history, ask for a prediction; zero-shot alone loses to naive baselines. (2) Fine-tuning — adapt weights (often LoRA), selective loss masking; consistently beats prompting. (3) Frozen encoder + adapter — LLM stays frozen, a light adapter maps into the KT backbone; LLM enriches, KT tracks time. (4) Retrieval-augmented (RAG) — pull evidence at inference (similar learners, subgraphs); widens the cold-start lead. The trend: from replacing the KT backbone → to augmenting it.
    - Finding 3 · RQ4 — Does it actually work? What holds up: beats conventional baselines on AUC; largest gain in cold-start / sparse data (most replicated result); fine-tuning > prompting; hybrid > either part alone. What doesn't: LLM alone loses to a lightweight DKT (AUC 0.69 zero-shot, 0.77 fine-tuned, vs 0.83 for DKT); dialogue-based KT is weakest (AUC 0.66–0.77); gains range 1–4 points and are condition-dependent. Verdict: evidence supports "LLMs can improve KT", not "LLMs can replace KT".
    - Finding 4 · RQ3 — The gap that matters most: ZERO of the 44 studies measure learning outcomes. No learning gains, no retention, no transfer. Only 4 of 44 are truly pedagogically grounded (they produce an instructional artefact). Pedagogical words appear in motivations, never in dependent variables. Most studies stop at a probability number — no hint, no item, no report. Bottom line: we can predict better; we have no idea whether students learn better.
    - Finding 5 · RQ4 — Can we trust the numbers? AUC dominates; calibration (the actual probability) is rarely reported. Non-comparable baselines (same dataset — ASSISTments, EdNet — but different filtering → different numbers). Temporal incoherence: mastery can drop after a correct answer, with no accepted metric or fix. Thin coverage: mostly single-dataset, cross-platform claimed but rarely tested, explanations scored for plausibility rather than faithfulness. Why it matters: the decision to advance a learner depends on the absolute probability, not its rank.
    PART 5 — CONCLUSION:
    - The field in one slide: KT tracks mastery per concept, predicts the next answer, and answers "what should come next?"; LLMs add reading of questions and conversations, working on brand-new content, and explaining/generating practice; learners feel it as right difficulty at the right time, less boredom and dropout, and feedback on the missing prerequisite. Mnemonic: KT = the model of the learner · LLMs = the language it can finally read.
    - One sentence to remember: "LLMs transform knowledge tracing from a black-box prediction into a transparent, actionable tool that can finally support personalized learning at scale."
